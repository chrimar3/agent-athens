You push through the door into a basement that smells of vinyl and wood. Record crates line the walls on your left; to your right, a small performance space where the acoustic treatment turns every scrape of a bow into something deliberate. Eighty people at capacity. Tonight there are maybe fifty chairs set out, and the performer is close enough that you can see rosin dust lift off the strings.

Joanna Mattrey is a New York-based violist, composer, and improviser whose work sits at the boundary between contemporary classical and free improvisation. She studied at the New England Conservatory and has performed alongside Tyshawn Sorey, Henry Threadgill, Marc Ribot, John Zorn, and Elliott Sharp — musicians who treat improvisation as architecture, not decoration. Her solo albums "Veiled" and "Dirge," both released on independent labels, explore the viola through extended techniques, electronic alterations, and compositions that function as frameworks for real-time decision-making. She is also a certified Alexander Technique teacher, which shapes how she approaches the physical act of playing — the instrument becomes an extension of breath and posture, not just fingers and bow.

Underflow draws a crowd that knows what it is coming to hear. People arrive early and flip through the record crates by the entrance, pulling out releases on Relative Pitch and Tzadik to check the liner notes. A few musicians from the Athens improvisation scene take the front row — close enough to watch the bowing technique — and you can see them studying the performer's hand position between pieces. Conversation happens before and after, not during. The silence in this room is a collective decision.

A Mattrey performance does not follow the arc of verse-chorus-verse. Expect extended passages where the viola produces sounds you did not know the instrument contained — harmonics that shimmer, percussive bowing that cracks like static, sustained drones that settle into the room like weather. The compositions leave space for improvisation, and the improvisations carry compositional weight. Pieces unfold rather than progress.

If you want melody-driven music or a social evening with conversation and drinks, Underflow on a night like this asks for something different — stillness, attention, and patience with sounds that build on their own time. But if you are the kind of listener who finds a single sustained note more interesting than a chord progression, and you want to hear an improviser who has shared stages with John Zorn and Henry Threadgill working in a room this small, this is why you came to Koukaki tonight.

Underflow is in the Koukaki neighborhood, a short walk from Syngrou-Fix metro station on the Red line. Doors at 21:00. Tickets are €10 — for a performer who sells out ISSUE Project Room in New York, this is Athens pricing at its most generous. The venue doubles as a record store, so arrive early to browse. Cash and cards accepted. The room fills from the front, and the front rows offer a proximity to the instrument that changes what you hear.

An international improviser of Mattrey's caliber playing a room this small only happens because Athens' experimental scene operates at a scale where intimacy is still possible. Underflow programs roughly one show like this per week. They are rarely repeated.
