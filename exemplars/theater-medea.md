The marble figures watch from their pedestals as you enter a room that was built to hold sculpture, not audiences. Footsteps echo off stone floors. The air carries the cool, mineral stillness of a gallery after hours, and somewhere behind you, a counter-tenor's voice begins to fill the space.

Mideia Exodos III is a site-specific theater work staged inside the National Glyptotheque in Goudi, among the permanent collection of Greek sculptor Yianoulis Halepas. Director Konstantinos Chatzis — founder of the Chroma theater company, trained in the physical theater traditions of Lecoq and Brook — has built a dramaturgical synthesis that places Euripides' Medea text in direct conversation with Halepas' sculptures. The music of Giorgos Koumendakis anchors the production: his 1991 composition "Eros Demon," written for counter-tenor and two pianos on the poetry of Sappho, becomes the emotional spine of the performance. Koumendakis composed the music for the 2004 Athens Olympic ceremonies; here his work operates at chamber scale, winding through gallery rooms rather than filling a stadium.

Before the performance, the audience drifts through the gallery rooms where the Halepas sculptures stand. People pause in front of the marble figures, lean toward the pedestals, talk quietly about whether the pieces they are looking at will appear in the production. Some have brought the Glyptotheque's own exhibition guide and are reading it against what they know of the Medea text. When the lights shift and the performers begin, the audience arranges itself standing along the gallery walls — there are no assigned seats — and the spacing between bodies becomes part of the composition. The room asks for stillness, and the audience gives it.

The production moves through the gallery rooms as a living installation. Euripides' text follows the arc of a refugee woman — Medea as exile — while the Halepas sculptures around you become silent witnesses to her displacement. The counter-tenor's voice fills corridors designed for contemplation, not performance. Each room shifts the acoustic, so the experience changes as you move with the production through the space.

If you prefer traditional proscenium theater with clear sightlines and comfortable seating, this is not that. But if you are drawn to the intersection of ancient text, contemporary music, and physical space — where the venue itself becomes a character — this production uses the Glyptotheque in a way that cannot be replicated elsewhere.

The venue is at Alsos Stratou (Army Park) in Goudi, with the entrance off Katechaki Avenue. Katechaki metro station on the Blue line puts you within a ten-minute walk. Performances run Wednesday through Sunday from February 25 to March 15. Arrive early to orient yourself in the gallery — the sculptures you see before the performance begins are the same ones that frame the action. The space is not heated to gallery standards, so bring a layer in February.

No other theater production in Athens this season puts Euripides inside a sculpture museum with a Koumendakis score performed live among the works of Halepas. The combination exists here, for three weeks, and then it does not.
