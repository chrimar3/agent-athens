The London Philharmonic Orchestra performs at the Christos Lambrakis Hall in Megaron Mousikis on March 7, with Paavo Jarvi conducting and Alexandre Kantorow at the piano. Nineteen hundred and sixty seats rake upward, a 6,080-tube pipe organ fills the back wall, and the room holds the silence before a hundred musicians breathe in together.

Jarvi holds the music directorship of Zurich's Tonhalle Orchestra and has led the Deutsche Kammerphilharmonie Bremen for two decades. Gramophone and Diapason both named him Artist of the Year in 2016. He carries the Sibelius Medal, awarded for deepening the reach of the Finnish composer's music worldwide. Kantorow, the French pianist, in 2019 became the first French musician ever to take the Gold Medal at the International Tchaikovsky Competition. He was twenty-two. He won it playing the very concerto he performs here.

The audience for a Saturday evening orchestral concert at the Lambrakis Hall assembles by program: subscribers who hold season tickets and mark their calendars by conductor, piano students who want to watch Kantorow's hands from the stalls, couples who attend the Megaron for occasions that warrant the foyer.

The program opens with Tchaikovsky's Piano Concerto No. 2 in G major — not the famous First that packs every season, but the rarely programmed Second that most concertgoers have never heard live. The central movement shifts into something closer to chamber music: the piano yields to extended solos from the concertmaster's violin and the principal cello, turning the stage briefly into a triple concerto. This is the work Kantorow chose at the Tchaikovsky Competition while every other finalist played No. 1. After the interval, Jarvi leads Sibelius's Symphony No. 2 in D major — a work that builds from quiet fragments into one of the most sustained orchestral climaxes in the repertoire. Expect roughly two hours and twenty minutes including intermission.

If you prefer intimate settings where you hear a bow touch a string from three metres away, the Lambrakis Hall at full capacity with a hundred musicians on stage is a different animal. But if you want to feel the floor shift when a symphony orchestra opens up at full volume, this is one of the few nights a year Athens gets that.

The Megaron is directly above Megaro Moussikis metro, one stop from Syntagma. Seven ticket tiers run from €25 to €110 — upper circle carries the sound cleanly, stalls centre offers direct sightlines to Kantorow's hands. Arrive early enough to walk the foyer: 2,500 square metres of open space with views toward Lycabettus.

A touring orchestra of this caliber, a conductor who owns Sibelius, and a pianist returning to the concerto that made his name — all in a hall built to hold every note.
