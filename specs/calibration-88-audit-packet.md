## 8fadb917323e786c — Animal Farm (Megaron) — full-8-section, 541w, 98/100

I Farma ton Zoon — I Parastasi Archizei is a children's theater production at the Musical Library of Megaron Mousikis in Ambelokipi, Athens, running through May 2026. The Yiannis Marinos Hall seats children low enough that every puppet, every pratfall, every conspiratorial glance from the actors lands at eye level.

George Orwell published Animal Farm in 1945 as a political allegory. Director Michalis Koilacos and co-adaptor Panos Christodoulou strip the Cold War framework and rebuild the story around something a four-year-old can grasp: what happens when one person makes all the rules. Their Mr. Jones converts his farm into a circus, promising the animals fame and applause while pocketing the proceeds. Rock turkeys, a cannon-shooting chicken, pigs wallowing through choreographed mud — the spectacle is deliberately absurd, pitched to hold the attention of audiences who have no obligation to sit still. When the animals discover the deception and revolt, the production turns the decision-making over to the room. After two consecutive years of sold-out runs at the Musical Library, I Farma ton Zoon returns for a third season, its staying power built on the fact that the interactive structure genuinely changes each performance.

The audience splits into two visible camps: parents who came for Orwell and children who came for the chicken. Both groups end up on their feet. The hall fills with families who treat Sunday-morning theater at the Megaron as a routine — stroller parking in the lobby, coffee from the ground-floor cafe, seats chosen for sightline rather than status.

Anna Sapka's set and costumes turn three actors into an entire farmyard. Angeliki Tombrou, Tassos Antoniou, and Giannis Pliakis cycle through roles with puppet work by Dimitra Konstantinidou filling the gaps — a goat here, a rooster there, each one distinct enough that children shout warnings when Mr. Jones approaches. Vasilis Tzavaras composed the live score, with lyrics by Elissaios Vlachos, and the music carries the narrative through transitions that would otherwise lose a three-year-old. At seventy minutes, the runtime respects the limits of its youngest audience members without rushing the story's turn from comedy to collective problem-solving.

If you want a quiet afternoon where children sit and absorb, this is not that production. The actors ask questions, wait for answers, and follow where the room leads. But if you want your children to experience the mechanics of democracy — voting, disagreeing, compromising — inside a story that makes those abstractions physical and loud, I Farma ton Zoon delivers that lesson without ever calling it a lesson.

The Megaron is directly above Megaro Moussikis metro. Tickets run from nine to thirteen euros through the Megaron box office or webtics.megaron.gr. The Yiannis Marinos Hall inside the Musical Library is configured specifically for young audiences, with peripheral sightlines designed so that even the smallest viewers see the full stage. Arrive ten minutes early — seating is assigned but families with very young children cluster near the aisles for quick exits if needed. Performances run Sundays at 11:30, with additional weekday school showings.

Two years of sold-out houses and a third season at one of Athens' premier cultural institutions — I Farma ton Zoon has earned its place not by prestige but because children drag their parents back.

## e344a237f2e52577 — Easter Cantatas (Megaron) — full-8-section, 486w, 98/100

Paschalines Kantades sto Megaro is a concert at Megaron Mousikis in Athens, on 19 April 2026. Two guitars tune quietly in a room lined with music scores, and the first chord resolves into a harmony that predates amplification by three centuries.

The kantada arrived in Greece through the Ionian Islands during Venetian rule, a polyphonic vocal form built on primo-secondo-bass voicing where each line is sung by multiple voices rather than soloists. It migrated to Athens after the 1863 union of the Heptanese with Greece and became the dominant urban song form in the tavernas of Plaka and Monastiraki before rebetiko displaced it. Spyros and Makis Karaviotis, brothers from Kefalonia who formed their ensemble in 1997, have spent nearly three decades performing and archiving the tradition. They grew up learning Ionian harmonic polyphony from their grandmother Dorothea, a mandolinist, and their father, a baritone and Byzantine chanter. Their work extends beyond performance into preservation: they are digitizing the musical archives of Dionysios Apostolatos and Nikolaos Tsilimenos, Cephalonian maestros whose manuscripts would otherwise remain in private collections.

The audience for Paschalines Kantades sto Megaro skews toward people who know what a kantada is and want to hear it done properly. Choral society members, Heptanesian diaspora, couples who grew up with these melodies on their parents' records. The room gathers people who treat polyphonic singing as craft, not nostalgia.

The Teaching Hall of the Lilian Voudouri Music Library holds an intimate crowd inside the Megaron complex. The acoustics suit unamplified vocal harmony and mandolin. A male lyric choir and mandolin ensemble accompany the Karaviotis brothers, and the program moves from Easter hymns and sacred settings through to the lighter Athenian kantades that emerged when the tradition crossed from island to capital. The evening includes an award presentation to baritone Leonidas Antypas, recognizing a career spent inside this vocal tradition. The Stegi Ellinikon Chorodion, Greece's national choral foundation, organizes Paschalines Kantades sto Megaro with the stated ambition of establishing it as an annual Easter institution in the Athenian concert calendar.

If you want orchestral scale or amplified sound, this is voices and strings in a library hall. But if you want to hear a vocal tradition that carried Greek-language polyphony from Venice to Argostoli to Athens, performed by the musicians who have spent decades ensuring its notation survives, Paschalines Kantades sto Megaro is one of the few concerts in the city built for exactly that purpose.

Megaron Mousikis is on Vasilissis Sofias, directly adjacent to Megaro Moussikis metro. The concert begins at 20:30 in the Music Library's Teaching Hall, which sits inside the main complex. Tickets are available through ticketservices.gr. Arrive early enough to see the Lilian Voudouri Music Library itself, one of the largest music libraries in southern Europe, which shares the building.

Three centuries of kantada tradition, one evening, and a room small enough that every voice reaches you without a speaker between.

Rouggeri, who founded the Children's Stage at both the National Theater and the National Lyric Stage in the 1990s, structures the show as a chain of origin stories: Earth and Sea splitting from void, Zeus overthrowing Kronos, Prometheus stealing fire, Athena winning Athens from Poseidon. If your children fidget in intimate spaces, the scale here works in your favor. If you prefer cozy, this is not that.
